Title: Biological pest control
Category: pest
Source: Organic Farming Guide educational knowledge base

Biological control uses predators, parasitoids, and disease organisms of pests. Many are already in the field: ladybird beetles, spiders, lacewings, dragonflies, and tiny parasitic wasps. The first task is to stop killing them with broad sprays. The second is to give them nectar, unsprayed refuge, and time. Deliberate releases, such as Trichogramma wasps against moth eggs or a Bacillus thuringiensis product against caterpillars, are additional tools where a local supplier and an approved product exist.

Benefits:
- Works with the insects already on the farm
- Can be highly specific
- Reduces the need for sprays
- Supports pollination when refuges flower

Step-by-step instructions:
1. Learn to recognise at least five natural enemies on your crops.
2. Keep a flowering border such as coriander, marigold, or native weeds that are not a harvest problem.
3. Scout before any spray so you know whether predators are already increasing.
4. If you release Trichogramma or a similar insect, follow the supplier's timing for the pest's egg stage. A late release does nothing.
5. Use Bacillus thuringiensis only as a labelled product permitted by your organic standard, aimed at young caterpillars, and stored cool.

Required materials:
- A scouting notebook
- Flowering refuge plants
- A reputable biocontrol supplier when releases are planned

Practical examples:
- Ladybirds feeding on aphids in wheat
- Trichogramma releases timed to bollworm eggs in cotton
- Bt products against young tomato fruit worm where the certifier allows them

Related crops: Cotton, Tomato, Rice, Wheat

Precautions:
- A biocontrol insect released into a field that is sprayed the next day is wasted money.
- Microbial products have expiry dates and temperature limits.
- Not every wasp or beetle is beneficial. Identify before you act.

This is educational guidance drawn from widely described organic farming practice. Rates, varieties, and legal rules vary by soil, season, and country. Confirm important decisions with a local agriculture extension officer or your organic certifier.

Title: Botanical pesticides
Category: pest
Source: Organic Farming Guide educational knowledge base

Botanical pesticides are made from plants. Neem is the best known. Farmers also ferment or soak garlic and chilli, or use other local plants recommended by extension. These preparations can irritate skin and eyes, and some are toxic to fish and to beneficial insects. A traditional origin does not make a spray harmless. Use gloves, filter the liquid, and apply the weakest dilution that has worked in your own small test.

Benefits:
- Ingredients may be available on the farm or in the local market
- Usually break down faster than many synthetic pesticides
- Useful for small sucking-pest colonies
- Fit some organic standards when the recipe is allowed

Step-by-step instructions:
1. Choose a recipe your certifier and extension office accept. A simple garlic-chilli extract uses a handful of garlic and a few ripe chillies crushed in water, left overnight, filtered, and heavily diluted.
2. Add a drop of soap if the spray must spread on waxy leaves.
3. Strain it so sprayer nozzles do not clog.
4. Spray a test patch, wear protection, and apply in the evening.
5. Record the dilution. If it scorched the test patch, do not spray the field.

Required materials:
- Neem, garlic, or chilli
- Clean water
- Soap if needed
- Filter cloth
- Gloves and eye protection

Practical examples:
- A dilute garlic-chilli spray for aphids on a garden crop
- Neem oil for whiteflies when a soap emulsifier is used

Related crops: Chilli, Tomato, Wheat

Precautions:
- Chilli and garlic extracts burn eyes and broken skin.
- Keep all botanical concentrates away from children and labelled as sprays, not as food.
- Do not use tobacco extracts. Nicotine preparations are highly toxic and are not an acceptable organic practice in this guide.
- Never spray near a fish pond or a drinking-water source.

This is educational guidance drawn from widely described organic farming practice. Rates, varieties, and legal rules vary by soil, season, and country. Confirm important decisions with a local agriculture extension officer or your organic certifier.

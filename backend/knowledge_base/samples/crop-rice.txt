Title: Rice organic cultivation guide
Category: crop
Source: Organic Farming Guide educational knowledge base

Rice is a staple cereal that grows best in warm, humid conditions and soils that can hold standing water during the vegetative stage. Organic rice systems rely on puddled or well-levelled fields, green manure, and compost rather than synthetic nitrogen.

Suitable soil: Clay, clay loam, and alluvial soils that hold moisture. A near-neutral pH is preferred. Avoid very sandy fields unless water can be held by bunds.
Climate: tropical humid
Sowing season: Kharif, with nursery sowing at the start of the monsoon and transplanting when seedlings are about three to four weeks old. A rabi or summer crop is possible only where irrigation is reliable.
Water need: high

Organic fertilizers: Incorporate well-decomposed farmyard manure or compost before puddling. A green-manure crop such as dhaincha can be turned in before transplanting. Split applications of vermicompost or jeevamrutham are used as a microbial and nutrient supplement, not as a complete replacement for bulky organic matter.

Irrigation: Keep the field moist to shallowly flooded during tillering. Alternate wetting and drying can save water once the crop is established, but do not stress plants at flowering. Stop standing water as the grain hardens.

Natural pest management: Use resistant varieties where available, clip leaf tips of seedlings before transplanting, encourage spiders and dragonflies, and install perching sticks for birds. Neem oil or a permitted Bacillus thuringiensis product may be used for leaf-feeding caterpillars when monitoring shows a rising population. Remove grassy weeds that host stem borers.

Harvesting: Harvest when most grains are hard and golden and the panicle bends. Dry to a safe moisture level before storage and keep grain off damp floors.

This is educational guidance drawn from widely described organic farming practice. Rates, varieties, and legal rules vary by soil, season, and country. Confirm important decisions with a local agriculture extension officer or your organic certifier.

Title: Crop rotation
Category: general
Source: Organic Farming Guide educational knowledge base

Crop rotation is a planned sequence of crops on the same land. A useful sequence changes plant family, rooting depth, and nutrient demand. Cereals often follow legumes. Solanaceous vegetables such as tomato and chilli should not follow each other. A restorative green manure or a break crop is part of the rotation, not an interruption of it. Writing the sequence down is what separates a rotation from a series of last-minute choices.

Benefits:
- Breaks the cycle of soil-borne diseases and some insects.
- Uses nutrients left by the previous crop.
- Improves soil structure when deep-rooted and fibrous-rooted crops alternate.
- Spreads labour and market risk across the year.

Step-by-step instructions:
1. List the crops you can sell or eat and group them by family: cereals, legumes, solanaceae, cucurbits, alliums, and so on.
2. Place a legume before the hungriest crop when seed and water allow.
3. Avoid planting the same family in the same bed two seasons in a row.
4. Include a green manure or a weeding fallow if disease or nutsedge has become severe.
5. Match the sequence to rainfall. Do not plan an irrigated rabi crop you cannot water.
6. Review the plan after harvest and note which pests actually appeared.

Required materials:
- A seasonal calendar
- Seed for the next two crops
- Records from the previous season

Practical examples:
- Cowpea or mung, then wheat, then a summer green manure is a simple cereal-legume pattern.
- Tomato followed by onion or a cereal is safer than tomato followed by chilli.

Related crops: Wheat, Tomato, Chilli, Groundnut

Precautions:
- A rotation on paper fails if volunteer plants of the previous crop are left to host the same disease.
- Do not treat a legume as a complete fertilizer programme. It contributes nitrogen; it does not supply every nutrient.

This is educational guidance drawn from widely described organic farming practice. Rates, varieties, and legal rules vary by soil, season, and country. Confirm important decisions with a local agriculture extension officer or your organic certifier.

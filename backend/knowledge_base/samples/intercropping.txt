Title: Intercropping
Category: general
Source: Organic Farming Guide educational knowledge base

Intercropping places two or more crops in the same field at the same time. The combination works when the crops do not compete for the same space at the same moment. A tall cereal can protect a low legume. A fast crop can be harvested before a slow crop closes the canopy. Organic farmers also use intercrops and borders to confuse pests and to shelter natural enemies. Spacing must still leave room to weed and to harvest.

Benefits:
- More than one product from the same rainfall.
- Ground cover that shades weeds and protects soil.
- Sometimes fewer pests than in a large single-crop block.
- A legume intercrop can contribute nitrogen to the system.

Step-by-step instructions:
1. Choose a main crop and a companion with a different height or duration.
2. Set rows so the shorter crop still receives light. North-south rows are often easier to manage.
3. Sow the companion at the same time or at the stage recommended locally so one crop does not smother the other.
4. Weed early, because two crops make late weeding harder.
5. Harvest the early crop carefully so you do not uproot the remaining one.
6. Record the yield of both crops. An intercrop that ruins the main crop is not a success.

Required materials:
- Seed of both crops
- A marked spacing plan
- Mulch if the soil dries quickly

Practical examples:
- Groundnut with a row of pigeon pea uses the field after the groundnut is lifted.
- Turmeric grown in the light shade of young coconut or arecanut is a traditional multi-storey pattern in humid areas.
- Maize with cowpea covers the soil and supplies a pulse beside the cereal.

Related crops: Groundnut, Turmeric, Cotton, Sugarcane

Precautions:
- Two hungry crops planted at full density will both yield poorly.
- Mixed harvests must be dried and stored separately so one wet crop does not spoil the other.

This is educational guidance drawn from widely described organic farming practice. Rates, varieties, and legal rules vary by soil, season, and country. Confirm important decisions with a local agriculture extension officer or your organic certifier.

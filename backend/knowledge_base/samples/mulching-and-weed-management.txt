Title: Mulching and practical weed management
Category: soil
Source: Organic Farming Guide educational knowledge base

Mulch protects the soil surface from sun and heavy rain, while early weed control prevents young weeds from competing with the crop. Mulch material, thickness, and timing must fit the crop and local pest pressure. Weed management is most effective when several small actions are planned before weeds set seed.

Benefits:
- Slower moisture loss and less soil splash onto leaves.
- Reduced erosion and more stable soil temperature.
- Fewer weeds when mulch is clean and applied at the right stage.

Step-by-step instructions:
1. Remove established weeds before they set seed and keep perennial weeds from spreading by rhizomes or roots.
2. Use clean, dry plant residue that has not been treated with persistent herbicides or contaminated materials.
3. Apply mulch after the soil is moist and the crop is established, leaving an open ring around stems and trunks.
4. Use shallow cultivation near crop roots and hand-weed close to plants to avoid root damage.
5. Inspect under the mulch for slugs, rodents, or disease and adjust the material or depth if problems appear.
6. Add crop rotation, cover crops, and timely planting to reduce the next flush of weeds.

Required materials:
- Clean straw or leaf residue
- Hand hoe or suitable weeding tool
- Cover-crop seed where appropriate

Practical examples:
- A vegetable bed can be mulched after seedlings are established and the soil has been watered.
- A cereal-legume rotation can interrupt weed cycles that build up under repeated monocropping.

Related crops: Tomato, Chilli, Banana, Groundnut, Turmeric

Precautions:
- Do not place wet mulch against stems; trapped moisture can encourage rot.
- Avoid spreading weed seed heads or diseased crop residue as mulch.
- Some crops need a bare planting strip for warming or emergence; check local recommendations.

This is educational guidance drawn from widely described organic farming practice. Rates, varieties, and legal rules vary by soil, season, and country. Confirm important decisions with a local agriculture extension officer or your organic certifier.

Title: Organic fertilizers
Category: fertilizer
Source: Organic Farming Guide educational knowledge base

Organic fertilizers are materials whose nutrients become available as soil organisms break them down. Compost, farmyard manure, vermicompost, green manure, oil cakes, and microbial inoculants each do a different job. Bulky manures improve structure and supply a broad, slow release of nutrients. Cakes and some biofertilizers are supplements. A common mistake is to treat a liquid preparation as if it could replace several tonnes of organic matter on a cereal or sugarcane crop.

Benefits:
- Organic matter improves infiltration and water holding.
- Nutrients are released over time, which suits long-season crops.
- Legumes and biofertilizers can add nitrogen when they match the crop.
- Soil life that cycles nutrients is fed along with the crop.

Step-by-step instructions:
1. Identify the main limitation: low organic matter, poor nodulation, or a hungry previous crop.
2. Apply bulky compost or manure at land preparation so it is in the root zone.
3. Use a legume rotation or green manure where nitrogen is the main need.
4. Add oil cake or vermicompost as a smaller, more concentrated dressing near high-value crops.
5. Use biofertilizers as seed or soil treatments only when the product matches the crop, and follow the label.
6. Record what was applied, the area, and the crop response so the next season is based on observation.

Required materials:
- Compost
- Farmyard manure
- Vermicompost
- Neem cake or other oil cakes
- Green-manure seed
- A labelled biofertilizer if used

Practical examples:
- Wheat after cowpea needs less added nitrogen than wheat after another cereal.
- Banana pits are filled with compost because the plant cannot wait for a thin dusting of cake alone.

Related crops: Wheat, Banana, Sugarcane, Tomato

Precautions:
- Fresh poultry manure and fresh dung can scorch roots.
- Biofertilizers are living products. Heat, direct sun, and expired packets reduce their value.
- Certified farms must use only inputs allowed by their standard.

This is educational guidance drawn from widely described organic farming practice. Rates, varieties, and legal rules vary by soil, season, and country. Confirm important decisions with a local agriculture extension officer or your organic certifier.

Title: Seed selection and healthy nursery management
Category: seeds
Source: Organic Farming Guide educational knowledge base

A healthy crop begins with seed that suits the local season, soil, and market. Buy from a traceable source, check the variety and lot label, and avoid seed from diseased plants. For crops raised in nurseries, clean trays and a well-drained growing medium reduce damping-off. This guide gives general practices; use the local crop calendar for variety-specific timing.

Benefits:
- More even emergence and crop establishment.
- Fewer pests and diseases carried from the nursery into the field.
- Less seed and labour wasted on weak seedlings.

Step-by-step instructions:
1. Select a variety recommended by a local agricultural extension service for the season and intended use.
2. Check the seed label, lot, germination information, and any treatment instructions before opening the packet.
3. For direct-sown crops, prepare a fine, moist seedbed and sow at the depth and spacing recommended for that crop.
4. For nursery crops, clean trays and tools, use a porous seedling medium, and keep containers raised off contaminated soil.
5. Water gently so the medium stays moist but not saturated; provide light and airflow as seedlings emerge.
6. Remove weak or diseased seedlings and gradually expose sturdy seedlings to outdoor conditions before transplanting.
7. Transplant during a cool part of the day, water in, and protect seedlings from harsh sun or wind while they establish.

Required materials:
- Locally adapted seed
- Clean trays or a prepared seedbed
- Well-drained growing medium
- Clean water

Practical examples:
- Tomato and brinjal are commonly raised in a nursery and transplanted when sturdy.
- Maize and okra are commonly sown directly when soil is warm and moist.

Related crops: Tomato, Brinjal, Cabbage, Maize, Okra

Precautions:
- Do not reuse nursery trays without cleaning them.
- Do not sow seed into waterlogged or cold soil unless the crop is suited to those conditions.
- Follow the seed label and local regulations for any seed treatment; do not eat treated seed.

This is educational guidance drawn from widely described organic farming practice. Rates, varieties, and legal rules vary by soil, season, and country. Confirm important decisions with a local agriculture extension officer or your organic certifier.

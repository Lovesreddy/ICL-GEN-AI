Title: Soil preparation
Category: soil
Source: Organic Farming Guide educational knowledge base

Soil preparation in organic farming starts with the condition of the field rather than with a bag of fertilizer. The aim is a seedbed that roots can enter, that holds moisture without staying waterlogged, and that already contains decomposed organic matter. Walk the field after rain. Note where water stands, where the surface crusts, and where previous crops were weak. Those observations decide whether the first job is a drain, a bund, a load of compost, or simply timely ploughing.

Benefits:
- Better root growth and more even germination.
- Less erosion when residue and beds slow runoff.
- Higher water-holding capacity as organic matter increases.
- Fewer soil-borne problems when raw manure and waterlogging are avoided.

Step-by-step instructions:
1. Clear plastic, stones, and diseased crop residue. Healthy straw may be composted or used as mulch rather than burned.
2. Test soil when you can. Note texture, pH, and whether a hard pan stops roots. Extension laboratories can do a basic test.
3. Plough or loosen only as deep as needed. Work soil when it is moist, not sticky and not dust-dry.
4. Spread well-decomposed compost or farmyard manure and incorporate it in the top soil. Do not apply fresh dung against seed.
5. Level fields for rice and make raised beds or ridges for vegetables, turmeric, and groundnut where drainage is poor.
6. Form bunds on slopes, add a green-manure crop if the calendar allows, and prepare a fine but not powdery seedbed.

Required materials:
- Compost or decomposed farmyard manure
- Green-manure seed where a window exists
- Field implements
- Soil-test report if available

Practical examples:
- A tomato bed on sandy loam receives two to three centimetres of compost worked into the top 15 centimetres, then a straw mulch after transplanting.
- A rice field that crusts is puddled only after a dhaincha crop has been turned in, instead of relying on fertilizer at transplanting.

Related crops: Rice, Tomato, Groundnut, Turmeric

Precautions:
- Working wet clay compacts the soil for the whole season.
- Deep ploughing on a steep slope without bunds increases erosion.
- Raw manure can burn seedlings and may carry weed seeds and pathogens.

This is educational guidance drawn from widely described organic farming practice. Rates, varieties, and legal rules vary by soil, season, and country. Confirm important decisions with a local agriculture extension officer or your organic certifier.

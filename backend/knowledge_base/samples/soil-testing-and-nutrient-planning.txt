Title: Soil testing and organic nutrient planning
Category: soil
Source: Organic Farming Guide educational knowledge base

A soil test is a starting point for nutrient decisions, not a complete diagnosis of crop health. Samples must represent the field, and results should be interpreted with the crop, yield goal, irrigation water, and organic-standard requirements in mind. Compost and rotations build soil condition over time, while a test helps identify constraints that may need specific attention.

Benefits:
- Fewer unnecessary amendments and lower input costs.
- Earlier detection of pH, salinity, or nutrient constraints.
- A repeatable record for comparing fields and seasons.

Step-by-step instructions:
1. Divide land into sampling areas with similar soil, slope, crop history, and management.
2. Collect a representative sample from each area using the laboratory's clean tools, depth, and sampling instructions.
3. Label samples clearly and tell the laboratory which crop is planned and whether the field is irrigated.
4. Review the report with a qualified local adviser; do not treat a single result as a diagnosis of a leaf symptom.
5. Prioritize mature compost, cover crops, and rotation, then add only amendments that are permitted and supported by the report.
6. Keep the test, input receipts, application dates, and crop response together; retest at an interval advised by the laboratory.

Required materials:
- Clean sampling tool
- Field map or notes
- Laboratory sample bags
- Recent crop and amendment history

Practical examples:
- Separate a low-lying field from a well-drained field rather than mixing both into one sample.
- Compare soil-test trends over multiple seasons before changing a long-term nutrient plan.

Related crops: Rice, Wheat, Tomato, Groundnut, Cotton

Precautions:
- Do not sample immediately after applying an amendment unless the laboratory specifically advises it.
- Do not apply lime, gypsum, or micronutrients based only on a photograph or a generic online schedule.
- Check that every input is allowed by the farm's certification standard before application.

This is educational guidance drawn from widely described organic farming practice. Rates, varieties, and legal rules vary by soil, season, and country. Confirm important decisions with a local agriculture extension officer or your organic certifier.

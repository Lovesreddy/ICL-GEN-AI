Title: Sustainable farming techniques
Category: general
Source: Organic Farming Guide educational knowledge base

Sustainable organic farming is a set of habits: keep the soil covered, keep living roots in the ground when possible, diversify crops, recycle nutrients, use water deliberately, and write down what happened. No single product creates a sustainable farm. The techniques on this site — compost, rotation, intercropping, mulch, and monitored pest control — work together. A farmer who adopts all of them in one week may fail from labour overload. A farmer who adds one practice each season and measures it will learn what fits the land.

Benefits:
- Yield that depends less on a single purchased input.
- Soil that is more resilient to a missed rain.
- Lower environmental load on streams and beneficial insects.
- Knowledge that stays on the farm when prices of inputs change.

Step-by-step instructions:
1. Pick one weak point from last season: crusted soil, a repeated disease, or a water shortage.
2. Choose one practice that addresses it and that you have the labour to finish.
3. Apply it on part of the field if you are unsure, and leave a comparison strip.
4. Record sowing date, inputs, rainfall or irrigations, pests, and yield.
5. Keep what worked and drop what only added work.
6. Share the result with neighbouring farmers and the extension officer, including the failures.

Required materials:
- A notebook
- Seed and compost for the chosen practice
- A comparison area in the same field

Practical examples:
- Mulching half a chilli field shows whether the water saving is worth the labour of gathering straw.
- A three-crop rotation written on the wall of the store is more useful than a long list of practices remembered only at sowing time.

Related crops: Chilli, Wheat, Cotton, Banana

Precautions:
- Copying a practice from a wet district into a dry district without changing the variety or the spacing often fails.
- Sustainability claims on a label still require certification if the word organic is used for sale.

This is educational guidance drawn from widely described organic farming practice. Rates, varieties, and legal rules vary by soil, season, and country. Confirm important decisions with a local agriculture extension officer or your organic certifier.

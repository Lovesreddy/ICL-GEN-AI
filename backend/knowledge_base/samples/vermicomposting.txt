Title: Vermicomposting
Category: compost
Source: Organic Farming Guide educational knowledge base

Vermicomposting uses surface-feeding worms, most often Eisenia fetida or a similar epigeic species, to consume partly decomposed organic matter. The castings are fine, dark, and rich in plant-available nutrients and microbial activity. Worms are not a substitute for a large compost programme on cereals, but they are valuable for nurseries, vegetables, and side-dressing. Worms need bedding, moderate moisture, and protection from heat, ants, and waterlogging. They do not thrive in a fresh, heating manure pile.

Benefits:
- Produces a fine amendment suited to nurseries and planting holes.
- Reduces kitchen and farm vegetable waste.
- Improves soil aggregation when used regularly.
- Can be made in small beds close to the homestead.

Step-by-step instructions:
1. Build a bed, tank, or windrow in full shade with a floor that drains. A common farm bed is about one metre wide and 30 to 45 centimetres deep.
2. Add moist bedding: decomposed dung, leaf compost, coconut coir, or shredded cardboard. The material should already have passed its hot stage.
3. Introduce a known worm culture. A starting density often discussed in extension guides is several hundred adult worms per square metre, increased as the farmer gains experience.
4. Feed in thin layers of chopped vegetable waste, pre-composted dung, and spent crop residues. Avoid meat, oil, citrus in large amounts, and fresh poultry manure.
5. Keep the bed as moist as a wrung sponge. Cover it with wet sacking or leaves. Protect it from chickens, ants, rats, and direct sun.
6. After about six to eight weeks, stop feeding a section so worms move toward fresh food, then harvest the finished castings. Dry them in shade and screen out remaining worms.

Required materials:
- Epigeic earthworms
- Shade and a drained bed
- Pre-decomposed dung or compost
- Vegetable waste
- Water and a breathable cover

Practical examples:
- A tomato grower mixes one or two handfuls of vermicompost into each planting hole and keeps the rest of the fertility in a basal compost dressing.
- A school garden feeds the worm bed with canteen vegetable peelings and uses the castings for potting mix, blended with soil and coarse compost.

Related crops: Tomato, Chilli, Turmeric, Banana

Precautions:
- Hot, fresh dung will kill worms. Pre-compost it until it no longer heats.
- Too much water drives air out of the bed and the worms leave or die.
- Castings that heat up in a sealed bag were not finished. Dry and cure them in the shade.

This is educational guidance drawn from widely described organic farming practice. Rates, varieties, and legal rules vary by soil, season, and country. Confirm important decisions with a local agriculture extension officer or your organic certifier.

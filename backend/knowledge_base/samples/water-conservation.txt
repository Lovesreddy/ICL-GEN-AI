Title: Water conservation
Category: water
Source: Organic Farming Guide educational knowledge base

Water conservation on an organic farm is mostly soil management. Organic matter and mulch let rain enter and then slow its evaporation. Bunds, trenches, and a farm pond keep water on the land. Irrigation is timed to the stages when the crop actually forms yield, instead of keeping the surface wet every day. Rice can use alternate wetting and drying after establishment. Vegetables do better with drip or furrow water at the root than with a hose on the leaves.

Benefits:
- Longer gaps between irrigations on mulched soil.
- Less erosion and more groundwater recharge from slowed runoff.
- Fewer leaf diseases when foliage stays dry.
- Lower pumping cost.

Step-by-step instructions:
1. Repair bunds and field channels before the monsoon.
2. Add compost so the soil can hold what the rain delivers.
3. Mulch wide-spaced crops as soon as the soil is moist.
4. Level rice fields so a shallow water layer is even, and consider alternate wetting and drying after tillering if weeds are under control.
5. Use drip or pitchers for orchards and vegetables where equipment or labour allows.
6. Irrigate at the sensitive stages: crown-root initiation in wheat, flowering and boll fill in cotton, pegging in groundnut, fruit swell in tomato.

Required materials:
- Mulch
- Compost
- Bunds or trenches
- Drip lines or watering cans
- A way to watch soil moisture

Practical examples:
- Banana basins covered with chopped leaves need less frequent filling than bare basins in the same week.
- A groundnut field that is over-irrigated stays leafy and develops more leaf spot than a field watered at flowering and pod fill.

Related crops: Rice, Banana, Groundnut, Tomato

Precautions:
- Alternate wetting and drying is not suitable for a field that already cracks and stresses the crop at flowering.
- Mulch against the stem of a seedling can keep the crown too wet.
- A farm pond needs a safe edge and should not receive runoff from a pesticide store or livestock yard.

This is educational guidance drawn from widely described organic farming practice. Rates, varieties, and legal rules vary by soil, season, and country. Confirm important decisions with a local agriculture extension officer or your organic certifier.
